Ransomware in 2026: Bigger Targets, Smarter Attacks

Criminal organisations now run ransomware like a business — complete with customer support, refund policies, and profit-sharing. We investigate how the threat evolved, who is behind today's biggest attacks, and what individuals and organisations can do to protect themselves.

By Nimasha Fernando | June 2026 | 7 min read

What is Ransomware?

Ransomware is a type of malicious software that encrypts a victim's files, making them inaccessible until a ransom is paid — usually in cryptocurrency, which is difficult to trace. What began as clumsy attacks by individual hackers in the early 2010s has evolved into a sophisticated global criminal industry worth billions of dollars per year.

Modern ransomware operations are structured like legitimate businesses. There are developers who write the malware, affiliates who deploy it, negotiators who handle victim communications, and even "customer support" teams that help victims who want to pay but don't know how to buy cryptocurrency.

Ransomware-as-a-Service: Crime Goes Corporate

The biggest shift in recent years is the rise of Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS). Criminal groups build and maintain ransomware platforms, then rent access to other criminals who carry out the actual attacks. Profits are split: typically 80% to the affiliate who deployed the attack, 20% to the platform operator.

This model democratised cybercrime. You no longer need to be a skilled programmer to launch a devastating ransomware attack. You just need to rent the right tools and find an unpatched network. The barrier to entry collapsed, and the volume of attacks surged.

"Ransomware is no longer a hacker's toy. It is a billion-dollar criminal industry with supply chains, HR, and customer service."

Who Gets Targeted?

In the early days, ransomware targeted individuals at random. Today, attacks are carefully planned against high-value targets. Hospitals are particularly attractive because downtime can cost lives, creating enormous pressure to pay quickly. Schools and universities hold vast stores of personal data. Critical infrastructure — power grids, water treatment facilities, port logistics systems — is valuable because disruption affects millions.

Supply chain attacks have become a preferred vector. Rather than attacking a large, well-defended company directly, hackers compromise a smaller software vendor or IT provider that the company trusts. The SolarWinds attack of 2020 used this approach to breach thousands of government and corporate networks simultaneously.

Common Entry Points for Ransomware Attacks

  • Phishing emails with malicious attachments or links
  • Unpatched software vulnerabilities and outdated systems
  • Weak or reused passwords without multi-factor authentication
  • Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) exposed to the internet
  • Compromised third-party vendors and supply chain partners
  • Malicious advertisements on legitimate websites (malvertising)

The Double Extortion Twist

Traditional ransomware encrypted files and demanded payment to restore them. Organisations with good backups could refuse to pay and recover on their own. Criminals adapted. Today, most major ransomware operations use double extortion: before encrypting the victim's files, attackers first steal copies of sensitive data. They then threaten to publish it publicly on the dark web if the ransom isn't paid.

This means backups are no longer enough. Even an organisation with perfect backups faces the threat of customer data, trade secrets, or employee records being posted online. The reputational and regulatory consequences can exceed the ransom demand itself.

How to Defend Against Ransomware

No defence is perfect, but layered security dramatically reduces risk. Experts consistently recommend a combination of technical controls and human training, because the most sophisticated firewall in the world can be bypassed by one employee clicking a convincing phishing email.

Protection Checklist

  1. Enable multi-factor authentication on all accounts
  2. Keep your operating system and all software up to date
  3. Back up important files regularly to an offline drive
  4. Never open email attachments from unknown senders
  5. Use a password manager and never reuse passwords
  6. Segment your network so a breach cannot spread freely
  7. Train staff regularly to recognise phishing attempts

What Happens When You Get Hit?

If an organisation is breached, the immediate priority is containment: isolating infected systems to stop the ransomware spreading across the network. Law enforcement agencies including Interpol, the FBI, and the UK's National Crime Agency advise against paying ransoms — both because payment funds future attacks and because there is no guarantee attackers will provide working decryption keys.

In recent years, international cooperation has achieved some wins. LockBit's infrastructure was seized by law enforcement in early 2024, disrupting one of the world's most prolific ransomware operations. But new groups emerge quickly to fill the gap. Staying ahead requires constant vigilance.
